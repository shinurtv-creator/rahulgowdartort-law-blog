When garbage becomes a problem: the issue we ignore every day

By Rahul Gowda R, first-year law student

Every morning I see it. A pile of trash next to a road. A plastic bag stuck in a drain. A public bin overflowing onto the sidewalk. Sometimes the smell reaches us before we even see the waste.

Most of us just walk by.

After learning about tort law I started seeing this issue in a different way. What if that pile of garbage is not a problem of cleanliness? What if it is a nuisance? Even more importantly, who is responsible for the harm it causes?

Garbage is not just dirty

When we hear the word “nuisance” we usually think of something loud, like sounds, smoke, bad smells or something blocking our way. In tort law however, nuisance has a serious legal meaning.

A nuisance can involve an interference with the use or enjoyment of land in the case of private nuisance, while public nuisance involves interference with rights or interests that belong to the public.

Garbage can easily fit into this category. Imagine living next to an open dump. The smell comes into your home every day. Flies and stray animals gather around it. During rain, waste flows into the street and drains. People cannot walk comfortably on the sidewalk.

At some point it stops being just “dirty.” It becomes an interference with life.

The law already considers nuisance

India’s current criminal law also gives us a useful connection.

Section 270, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 Public nuisance includes an act or illegal omission that causes injury, danger or annoyance to the public or to people living nearby, or to those using a public right. This rule has been in effect since 1 July 2024.

This is important because garbage left on a road is rarely a problem for just one person. It can affect pedestrians, shopkeepers, residents, drivers and sanitation workers at the same time.

A blocked sidewalk is not an inconvenience for one person. It can interfere with a right to walk. That is where an everyday pile of garbage starts to look very different from a legal perspective.

Who actually caused the problem?

This is where the issue gets complicated. Sometimes the person responsible is clear. A person throws a bag on the side of the road. Another adds a food wrapper. Someone else leaves construction waste there. Within days the same spot becomes a dump.

What happens when garbage is collected but not removed? What about a system that doesn’t work properly? What about waste that is moved without care?

These questions matter because tort law is ultimately about harm and responsibility.

The Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 place duties on local authorities regarding the collection, sorting, transportation, processing and disposal of municipal solid waste. Open dumping can also create public health issues.

So the problem cannot always be reduced to “people should stop littering.” There is a system behind every pile of garbage.

Bengaluru shows how real the problem is

This is not a classroom topic. Bengaluru has repeatedly had arguments about where and how the city’s waste should be handled.

In February 2026 residents near the Bellahalli waste-processing area blocked more than 150 garbage trucks, stopping waste disposal from parts of the city. Reports said Bengaluru produces about 6,000 tonnes of waste each day.

That event showed me something: garbage does not disappear when it leaves our doorstep. It simply moves elsewhere.

One neighborhood may want the garbage removed while another may not want to become the dumping ground. So the real question is not just “Where should we put the garbage?” It is: Who should take the consequences of the waste that an entire city produces?

When nuisance becomes a problem

The impact of unmanaged garbage is more than just an unpleasant smell. It can block roads and sidewalks. It can attract animals and insects. It can pollute the surroundings. It can make public spaces hard to use. When waste gets into drainage systems, a small issue can become a much bigger one during heavy rains.

This is why nuisance law is interesting. It takes something ordinary and asks a legal question: When does an inconvenience become legally unreasonable? That question has no single answer for every situation. Courts usually have to look at the nature, extent and circumstances of the interference.

The responsibility can’t belong to one side

I also think it would be unfair to put all the blame on municipal authorities. Citizens are part of the problem too. If waste is not sorted, if garbage is thrown on the road or if people deliberately create dump sites, the waste system becomes harder to manage.

At the same time, citizens cannot be expected to solve a city’s waste problem on their own. Responsibility has to work both ways.

Citizens must throw away waste properly. Authorities must manage the waste properly. That sounds simple. In practice it is surprisingly difficult.

What does tort law teach us here?

For me the interesting thing is that tort law is not only about paying after something goes wrong. It is also about seeing interference and asking who should take responsibility for harm.

Garbage makes this visible. A person may never think, “I am creating a nuisance.” They just throw a packet on the road. A municipal authority may not think, “We are interfering with someone’s enjoyment of property.” It may simply fail to clean a dump in time.

The person living next to that garbage does not experience it as a legal theory. They feel the smell. They feel the inconvenience. They feel the loss of a public space. That is where law meets life.

The question I keep thinking about

As a first-year law student I am starting to realize that some of the interesting legal problems are hiding in things we have gotten used to.

We have become so used to seeing garbage on the road that we sometimes stop questioning it. Maybe that is exactly when the law should make us stop.

If garbage can affect our health, property, movement and enjoyment of spaces, at what point should society stop calling it just “waste” and start calling it a legal nuisance?

Maybe the biggest nuisance is not the garbage itself. Maybe it is the idea that because we see it every day we have to accept it.
